Gates Foundation official says nutrition should be framed as a GDP statistic; WHO official says “no bigger risk than lack of action”.

Day 1 of the two-day international conference, ‘Act For Nutrition: The Emergency We Can No Longer Ignore’ — held at Islamabad’s Serena Hotel — concluded on Wednesday, with experts urging urgent action on Pakistan’s malnutrition and stunting crisis, with a focus on multisectoral interventions.

With more than 40 per cent of children under five stunted, a maternal mortality rate of 155 deaths per 100,000 live births, and malnutrition costing Pakistan upwards of $17 billion annually, the country stands at a crossroads: continue on the current trajectory and surrender an entire generation’s potential, or choose a different future.

The two-day conference is bringing together policymakers, international development leaders, health professionals and nutrition experts to confront the burgeoning crisis and build momentum for urgent, coordinated action.

The focus of discussions will be on moving the national conversation beyond statistics towards practical, measurable interventions that can improve nutrition outcomes and protect Pakis­tan’s human capital and future generations.

**5:15pm - Researcher calls for nutrition-related interventions at community, systemic levels**

Director at the International Research Force Dr Hana Mahmood said focus on nutrition was needed at the community, systemic, and legislative levels.

Remarking on awareness campaigns on the community level, she said, “Interventions are not designed according to the latest theories and latest approaches towards designing behaviour change”.

She stressed a multisectoral approach to nutrition, adding that “no specific industry owns nutrition”.

* * *

**5:00pm - Doctor stresses rigorous testing for pregnant women**

Dr Nosheen Ashraf of the National Institutes of Health, speaking about maternal nutrition, said that during antenatal visits, mothers are asked to take supplements to address nutritional deficiencies; however, she noted a lack of consistent monitoring.

Dr Ashraf said there were not enough laboratories in far-flung areas, as she called for rigorous testing of pregnant mothers to track any nutritional deficiencies.

* * *

**4:55pm - Gynaecologist stresses nutrition should not be limited to pregnancy or childbirth**

Capital Hospital Associate Gynaecologist Dr Urooj Yasir Khan said the focus of nutrition should not be limited to pregnancy or childbirth.

“There is hardly any awareness about nutrition in our adolescent girl groups.” Following a substantial loss of iron during menstruation, micronutrient deficiencies in the body at the onset of pregnancy, especially if it’s a teenage pregnancy, can lead to growth-restricted babies, she said.

Commenting on treatment for obesity, she said, “we don’t say what’s important to eat”, adding that the focus seems to be on the number of calories consumed.

* * *

**4:53pm - Founder Nutradevice calls for equitable access to nutritious food**

Founder Nutradevice Dr Nosheen Taqvi held that creating awareness about nutritious food did not guarantee nutrition; rather, she maintained that it was worth examining “if the mother can access it, implement it, afford it and keeping in view the power of the woman in the household, can she give it to the children”.

Dr Taqvi also clarified misconceptions around nutrition. “It is not true that healthy food is always expensive \[..\] as you don’t always have to focus on animal-based food”.

She also noted that mothers in Pakistani households did not prioritise themselves. “Food is given to the family first. and then the mother eats whatever is left of it.”

She also noted that stigma around weight gain was leading to nutritional deficiencies in women as well.

Founder Nutradevice Dr Nosheen Taqvi speaks at the conference. — White Star

**4:38pm - Researcher explains why ‘1,000-day’ period is critical to addressing malnutrition**

Director of the International Research Force Dr Hana Mahmood explained why the first 1,000-day period of an infant’s life remained critical in addressing malnutrition.

“The most important thing we have to attend to right now is that these 1,000 days are those days in which growth is exponentially high and the requirement for micronutrients is massive,” Dr Mahmood said.

She added that during this time, the immune system and major organs were developing in such a way that any lack in nutritional input could lead to irreversible damage.

“During the first two years of life, 80pc of a child’s brain develops,” she said. Dr Mahmood also noted that Pakistan had one of the highest child-under-five mortality rates, a majority of which was contributed by malnutrition.

“250m deaths can be averted by focusing on prevention of stunting,” she said.

**4:10pm - Agri consultant highlights need for demand pull in producing nutritionally rich food**

Agriculture consultant Hasan Khurrum Hanif, talking about producing nutritionally dense food, said, “Pakistani farmers need a demand pull, rather than a supply push”.

“Farmers will produce what you pay us to produce and if there is a market where I can sell zinc-fortified wheat, where I can sell vegetables with lower pesticide residue, all of those require a different input package, which has a cost component,” he said.

**3:55pm - Food safety infrastructure critical for nutrition, economy, says Tetra Pak executive**

Corporate Affairs Director at Tetra Pak Noor Aftab stressed the significance of food safety, saying that “food systems need to be treated as critical infrastructure”.

He said that Pakistan is one of the largest milk-producing countries, but there were no safe milk or pasteurisation laws in the country, depriving the economy of $30bn export potential.

He added that in Pakistan, milk is taxed at one of the highest rates in the world. “If there is a resilient food systems infrastructure built within Pakistan, coupled with sustainable fiscal roadmaps… that will allow \[…\] the infrastructure to act as a catalyst for nourishment and improvement.”

**3:45pm - Nutrition investment reduced absenteeism rate in Bangladesh, says official**

Project Manager at the Workforce Nutrition GM Reza Sumon, joining the conference through video conference from Bangladesh, discussed nutrition investments for the workforce.

He said that in Bangladesh’s government sector, the anaemia rate within the workforce was at 51.9pc. However, after interventions via funding for fortified rice, oils and iron and folic acid tablets, the anaemia rate dropped to 32pc, Sumon said, which helped convince the private sector to make similar investments in nutrition.

He said after the private sector invested $10m in nutrition in the past year, absenteeism in workplaces has reduced from 70pc to 40pc.

**3:40pm - EBM executive shares details on a fortified biscuits initiative**

Head of Corporate Communications & Sustainability at English Biscuit Manufacturers (EBM) Attiyah Inaam Khan shared details of an initiative launched by the company in which fortified biscuits were provided to mothers to address malnutrition.

“We tracked them for five years, and mothers who received biscuits showed great iron levels during pregnancy, and children born under the study showed stronger cognitive development,” she recalled.

“It is not about margins but more about whether there is evidence on the ground and whether the intervention can be scalable,” she added.

“Government can also play a very important lever here: tax credit for fortification in products and reduce the import duty on fortification,” she added.

**2:55pm - Nutrition not often framed as GDP statistic: Gates Foundation official**

Gates Foundation Senior Programme Officer of Development Policy and Finance Dr Ammar Malik said that according to World Bank statistics, for every dollar a government invested in nutrition, it could get up to $23 back in the form of economic benefits.

Questioning why such a high return on investment was still ignored, Malik said one of the issues was that nutrition investments were long-term, with returns becoming obvious years or decades later.

He added another issue was that nutrition is seen as a standalone topic in such discussions, instead of framing it as a gross domestic product statistic.

“If I were to say to you that Pakistan is losing 3pc of its GDP because of lack of investment in nutrition, that is a much stronger argument for economic policymakers than it is to talk about nutrition,” he said.

“The government of Pakistan can either now decide to spend more on nutrition today through our budgets, or we can pay the price of this lack of investment in the form of lost gross domestic product over the next several years or even decades,” he said.

**2:45pm — ‘Nutrition has few dedicated champions’**

“Why is this? Well, because malnutrition is multi-sectoral. It is the interaction of poor diet, poor water, poor sanitation, poor healthcare, and poor care in general, and so each of these areas has its own ministry.”

“There is no ministry of nutrition,” he added.

**9:55am - Dawn CEO says nutrition crisis does not ‘look the same’ across the country**

Dawn CEO Nazafreen Saigol Lakhani said the nutrition crisis did not “look the same in Tharparkar as it does in Chitral or in southern Punjab”, adding that the response could not be the same either.

“You will hear the economics of financing nutrition, the science of why children fail to grow even when food is technically available, and, I hope, the voices of the women and the mothers who carry the heaviest share of this burden and are so rarely asked to speak about it,” she said.

“With all those who have a role to play gathered around the same table, we have an opportunity to move from recognition of the scale of the problem towards urgent, coordinated and measurable action — action that can improve maternal and child nutrition, strengthen Pakistan’s human capital, and give future generations the opportunity to grow, learn and thrive,” she said.

“That is what this conference ultimately aims to achieve: to build a shared understanding of what needs to be done, and to begin charting a clear and practical roadmap for how we get there,” she added.

**9:53am - Dawn CEO says nutrition not a problem for health ministry to solve alone**

Dawn CEO Nazafreen Saigol Lakhani said that Pakistan loses close to $17 billion a year to the consequences of malnutrition, “more than what we typically borrow from the International Monetary Fund in any given year”.

“We spend enormous energy negotiating that borrowing. We spend comparatively little energy addressing a loss nearly as large, happening quietly, inside our own households,” she said.

“So this conference starts from a simple premise: nutrition is not a problem for a health ministry to solve alone. It sits at the intersection of health, agriculture, water, education, social protection, our economy and ultimately, our national security,” she added.

She said that countries such as Bangladesh, Peru, and Rwanda had demonstrated that stunting can be dramatically reduced within a generation, but “only when a nation decides, at the highest levels, that the future of its children is important enough to make it a collective priority”.

* * *

**9:52am - Dawn CEO calls stunting crisis ‘a national emergency’**

In her welcome remarks, Dawn CEO Nazafreen Saigol Lakhani said that four out of every ten children under the age of five in Pakistan were stunted.

“These are children whose physical growth and cognitive development may be permanently compromised – often before they have spoken their first words, entered a classroom or had the slightest chance to even dream of their own future,” she said.

“And when this is the reality for four out of every ten children in our country, we cannot merely call it a health problem. It is a national emergency – and a profound failure of our collective priorities,” she said.
